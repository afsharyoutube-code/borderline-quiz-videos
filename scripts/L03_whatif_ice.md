## Script
COLD OPEN (0:00–0:15) — Picture a world map, and now start raising the sea. One metre. Ten. Fifty. Coastlines crawl inland, and whole countries slip off the map. This would take thousands of years, not decades, but which countries would vanish, and which would survive? Let's melt the planet. Carefully.
VISUAL: real-shape world map, rising blue overlay with metre counter 0→10→50 m, small tag "THOUGHT EXPERIMENT · THOUSANDS OF YEARS", freeze on "?" over Europe/South Asia, logo sting.

CH1 How Much Ice Is Out There? — Before we drown anything, let's count the ice. According to National Geographic, there are more than five million cubic miles of ice on Earth [2]. Almost all of it sits in two giant ice sheets. Greenland's ice sheet covers about 80 percent of the island [4]. If it melted completely, it would raise global sea level by about 7.4 metres [3]. That's roughly the height of a two-storey house. Then there's Antarctica, the real heavyweight. Its ice could raise sea level by about 58 metres [3]. Add the world's mountain glaciers, from the Alps to the Himalayas, and you get less than half a metre more [5]. So what's the total? Scientists' numbers vary a little. NASA says the two ice sheets together would add nearly 65 metres [3]. The U.S. Geological Survey estimates about 70 metres if all glaciers and ice caps melted, and notes there's still some uncertainty about how much ice there really is [1]. So we'll call it roughly 65 to 70 metres. That's about as tall as a 20-storey building.
VISUAL: stacked bars Greenland 7.4 m / Antarctica ~58 m / glaciers <0.5 m; total "≈65–70 m" vs building silhouette; NASA/USGS tags.

CH2 How Long Would It Take? — Here's the first big reality check: this would not happen overnight, and not in our lifetimes. Some scientists estimate it would take more than 5,000 years to melt all of it [2]. The IPCC, the UN's climate science panel, gives a sense of the pace. Even if warming is held to 1.5 degrees Celsius, the seas would keep rising for centuries, with about 2 to 3 metres expected over the next 2,000 years. With 5 degrees of warming, that grows to roughly 20 metres over the same period [9] — still only about a third of a full melt. So the map you're about to see is a thought experiment. It's not a forecast for 2100. Think of it as a "worst case, given thousands of years" way of reading the world's geography.
VISUAL: timeline Today → 2100 → 2,000 yrs → 5,000+ yrs with shrinking ice cube; callouts; label "THOUGHT EXPERIMENT – NOT A 2100 FORECAST".

CH3 Gone Completely: The Island Nations — Let's start with the countries that would disappear entirely. The Maldives is the world's lowest-lying country. Its average ground level is about one and a half metres, and its highest natural point is only about 2.4 metres above the sea [14] — lower than a basketball hoop is from the floor. At 65 metres of sea-level rise, there's simply nothing left above water. All of its coral islands go under. Thousands of kilometres away in the Pacific, Tuvalu is in the same position. Its highest point is only about four and a half metres above sea level [17]. It too would vanish from the map. These are the clearest answers to our question: entire nations with nowhere higher to go.
VISUAL: Maldives chain highlighted then fades ("Max ≈ 2.4 m"); Tuvalu ("Max ≈ 4.5 m"); "COUNTRIES GONE" counter → 2.

CH4 Europe: Goodbye Lowlands — Now to Europe, starting with a country that already lives with the sea at its door: the Netherlands. According to the Dutch government's environmental assessment agency, about a quarter of the country lies below sea level, and nearly 60 percent is prone to flooding [13]. At 65 metres, Amsterdam, Rotterdam and The Hague are long gone. National Geographic's map shows the Netherlands disappearing [2]. But the elevation data tells a slightly different story: in the far southern corner, the Vaalserberg hill rises about 322 metres [18]. So almost all of the Netherlands would be lost, but a small hilly corner of the far south would still poke above the waves. Next door, Denmark. Mainland Denmark lies, on average, no more than about 30 metres above sea level [12]. On this map, most of the country would be gone [2]. Its highest hill, at about 173 metres [12], would become part of a scattering of islands. Head south to Venice. The lowest part of St Mark's Square is only about 55 centimetres above today's average sea level [16]. On this map, the Adriatic reclaims the city completely [2]. And on National Geographic's map, London is lost too [2].
VISUAL: Netherlands floods leaving dot "Vaalserberg ~322 m"; card "26% below sea level · 59% flood-prone (PBL)"; Denmark → islands ("highest ~173 m"); Venice pin under; London pin "NatGeo map".

CH5 Asia and Africa: Deltas Under Water — Asia has some of the world's largest river deltas, and many of the world's people live on them. Take Bangladesh. Roughly 80 percent of its land is fertile, low-lying river plain, and most of it sits less than 10 metres above sea level [11]. National Geographic's map shows all of Bangladesh underwater [2]. The elevation data suggests a small correction: a few peaks in the far south-east hills reach around 1,000 metres [11]. So most of Bangladesh would vanish, but those hilltops would survive. Next door, on this map, much of coastal India would flood [2]. And in China, National Geographic estimated that land home to about 600 million people when the map was made would be under water [2]. Africa, according to National Geographic, would lose less land than any other continent [2]. But that doesn't mean it escapes. On this map, Egypt's great cities of Alexandria and Cairo are swamped by the Mediterranean [2].
VISUAL: Ganges–Brahmaputra delta floods, SE hills dry ("hills ~1,000 m"); India & China coasts ("~600 million people, NatGeo est. 2013"); Africa barely changes except Nile Delta with Alexandria/Cairo pins.

CH6 The Americas and Australia — Across the Atlantic, North America's east coast takes a heavy hit. On National Geographic's map, the entire Atlantic seaboard disappears, along with the Gulf Coast and Florida [2]. Florida is a great geography trivia moment. It has the lowest "highest point" of any U.S. state: Britton Hill, just 105 metres high [15]. That means nearly all of Florida would vanish, but a sliver of high ground in the northern panhandle would technically stay dry. On the West Coast, San Francisco's hills would become islands, and San Diego would be gone [2]. In South America, Buenos Aires and coastal Uruguay would be wiped out, and so would most of Paraguay [2]. And Australia gets a strange new feature: a brand-new inland sea [2]. It would also lose much of the narrow coastal strip where more than four in five Australians live [19].
VISUAL: US east/Gulf coasts flood, Florida gone except "Britton Hill ~105 m"; SF islands; Río de la Plata, Paraguay; Australia inland sea + coastal fringe "85% live within 50 km of the coast (ABS)".

CH7 So What Would Be Left? — So, after all that ice is gone, what remains? The answer is: still most of the land on Earth. The mountains, high plateaus and continental interiors stay dry. Those coastlines are just redrawn, sometimes by hundreds of kilometres. The biggest losers are low places: atoll nations, river deltas and flat coastal plains. And that's where many of the world's big cities happen to be. The planet itself would be very different too. A world warm enough to melt all the ice might average around 27 degrees Celsius — according to National Geographic's estimate — compared with about 14 degrees in the late 20th century [2]. One important caveat: these maps simply raise the sea by about 65 metres everywhere. The real world would be more complicated, with land rising or sinking and the ocean not rising evenly. Think of them as a rough guide, not a precise forecast for your street.
VISUAL: new vs old coastline (dotted); Survives vs Lost split; tag "Simplified: sea raised ~65 m everywhere – real rise varies by region".

CH8 Reality Check: What Scientists Actually Expect by 2100 — Now let's come back to the real world, because this part matters. Nobody is predicting 65 metres by 2100. Realistic projections for this century are tens of centimetres to about a metre. The IPCC's latest assessment projects that global sea level will likely rise by about 28 to 55 centimetres by 2100 under very low emissions, and by about 63 centimetres to just over one metre under very high emissions, compared with 1995 to 2014 [9]. Under very high emissions, a rise approaching 2 metres by 2100 cannot be ruled out, but scientists have low confidence in it [9]. That's still a big deal for low-lying places. In the United States, for example, NOAA projects 10 to 12 inches of rise on average along the coast by 2050, about as much as the country saw over the previous 100 years, from 1920 to 2020 [10]. And moderate coastal flooding would happen more than ten times as often [10]. The full "all ice melted" world is a story of thousands of years. What happens this century is a much smaller number, but it still matters for millions of people living on the coast.
VISUAL: bars "0.28–0.55 m" (very low emissions) / "0.63–1.01 m" (very high), IPCC AR6, vs 1995–2014; dotted "approaching 2 m: can't be ruled out, low confidence"; zoom out vs 65 m bar; card "NOAA: US coast +10–12 in by 2050 (avg)".
